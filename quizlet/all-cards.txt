Trunk	The torso: the central axis of the body
Boundaries of the trunk	Inferior to the neck, superior to the lower limbs, and between the upper limbs
The 5 major subdivisions of the trunk	Thorax, Abdomen, Pelvis, Perineum, Back (dorsal trunk)
Thorax	The chest cavity: ribs, sternum, heart, lungs
Abdomen	The region between thorax and pelvis: the digestive tract
Pelvis	Inferior region within the pelvic girdle: bladder, rectum, reproductive organs
Perineum	Diamond-shaped region on the inferior pelvic floor: external genitalia and anus
Back (dorsal trunk)	Posterior region housing the vertebral column and spinal muscles
Anterior surface landmarks of the trunk	Sternal, pectoral, mammary, umbilical, inguinal
Posterior surface landmarks of the trunk	Vertebral, scapular, lumbar, sacral, gluteal
Sternal	Breastbone (anterior)
Pectoral	Chest (anterior)
Mammary	Breast (anterior)
Umbilical	Navel (anterior)
Inguinal	Groin (anterior)
Vertebral	Spine (posterior)
Scapular	Shoulder blade (posterior)
Lumbar	Lower back (posterior)
Sacral	Between the hips (posterior)
Gluteal	Buttock (posterior)
Superior vs. Inferior	Superior = towards the head; Inferior = towards the feet
Anterior (ventral) vs. Posterior (dorsal)	Anterior = front; Posterior = back
Medial vs. Lateral	Medial = closer to the midline; Lateral = further from the midline
Ipsilateral vs. Contralateral	Ipsilateral = same side of the body; Contralateral = opposite sides
Superficial vs. Deep	Superficial = closer to the skin surface; Deep = further inward
Parietal vs. Visceral	Parietal = lining the wall of a body cavity; Visceral = directly covering an internal organ
Median (midsagittal) plane	Vertical plane dividing the trunk into equal right and left halves
Sagittal plane	Vertical plane parallel to the median, dividing into unequal right and left parts
Coronal (frontal) plane	Vertical plane dividing into anterior and posterior parts
Transverse (axial) plane	Horizontal plane dividing into superior and inferior parts
What does the diaphragm separate?	The thoracic cavity from the abdominopelvic cavity
What does the thoracic cavity contain?	The pleural and pericardial cavities
Day 1 of development	Fertilization, in the ampullary region of the uterine tube
Where does fertilization occur?	The ampulla of the uterine tube: the widest part, near the ovary
Days 1 to 3 of development	Cleavage: mitotic divisions converting the single zygote into smaller cells
Blastomeres	The progressively smaller cells produced by cleavage of the zygote
Day 3 of development	Morula: a solid ball of 16 compacted cells, which enters the uterus
Morula	A solid ball of 16 compacted cells, formed on Day 3
Days 4 to 5 of development	Blastocyst: fluid enters to form a central cavity
Embryoblast (inner cell mass)	Located at one pole of the blastocyst; forms the embryo proper
Trophoblast (outer cell mass)	The outer epithelial wall of the blastocyst; forms the placenta
Day 6 of development	Implantation: the blastocyst attaches and embeds into the endometrium
Where does implantation occur?	The endometrium of the anterior or posterior uterine wall
Order of first-week events	Fertilization (D1), cleavage (D1 to 3), morula (D3), blastocyst (D4 to 5), implantation (D6)
Capacitation	A 7-hour conditioning period in the female reproductive tract that removes the glycoprotein coat and seminal plasma proteins from the acrosome
How long does capacitation take?	7 hours
Acrosomal reaction	After the sperm binds the zona pellucida, enzymes are released to penetrate the egg's coat
Cortical reaction	Triggered by sperm entry; prevents polyspermy
What prevents polyspermy?	The cortical reaction, triggered by sperm entry
Three events triggered by sperm entry	The cortical reaction (prevents polyspermy), completion of meiosis II, and restoration of the diploid chromosome count
Diploid chromosome number restored at fertilization	46
The two layers of the trophoblast	Cytotrophoblast (inner) and syncytiotrophoblast (outer)
Cytotrophoblast	Inner layer of mononucleated cells
Syncytiotrophoblast	Outer multinucleated zone without distinct cell boundaries; erodes maternal tissue and sinusoids, and secretes hCG
Which layer secretes hCG?	The syncytiotrophoblast
Which layer establishes primitive uteroplacental circulation?	The syncytiotrophoblast, by eroding maternal tissue and sinusoids
Bilaminar disc	Epiblast plus hypoblast, formed from the embryoblast in week 2
Epiblast	High columnar cells adjacent to the amniotic cavity; gives rise to ALL three primary germ layers
Hypoblast	Small cuboidal cells adjacent to the blastocyst cavity and yolk sac
Which layer gives rise to all 3 germ layers?	The epiblast
Gastrulation	Week 3: establishes all three germ layers by invagination through the primitive streak on the epiblast
The three germ layers	Ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm
EXAM TRAP: failure of the primitive streak to degenerate	Sacrococcygeal teratoma
Sacrococcygeal teratoma is caused by	Failure of the primitive streak to degenerate
Notochord	Midline cellular rod that provides structural support and induces the overlying ectoderm to undergo neurulation
Neurulation sequence	Neural plate, then neural groove, then neural tube
What does the neural tube form?	The central nervous system: brain and spinal cord
Neural crest cells	Detach during neural fold closure and migrate to form the PNS (sensory and autonomic ganglia), melanocytes, Schwann cells and the adrenal medulla
Ectoderm derivatives	Nervous system (CNS and PNS), epidermis of skin, hair, nails, sweat glands, adrenal medulla
Mesoderm derivatives	Bones, cartilage, connective tissue, muscle (skeletal, cardiac, smooth), cardiovascular system, kidneys, gonads
Endoderm derivatives	Epithelial lining of the GI tract, respiratory tract (trachea, lungs), liver, pancreas, urinary bladder
Paraxial mesoderm	Segments into somites: about 3 pairs per day, 42 to 44 pairs in total
How many somite pairs form?	42 to 44 pairs, at about 3 pairs per day
The three parts of a somite	Sclerotome, myotome, dermatome
Sclerotome	Bones and cartilage of the vertebral column
Myotome	Skeletal muscle
Dermatome	Dermis of the skin
Intermediate mesoderm	Forms the urogenital system: kidneys and gonads
Lateral plate mesoderm	Splits into a splanchnic (visceral) layer and a somatic (parietal) layer
Skeletal muscle origin	Paraxial mesoderm (somite myotomes) and the pharyngeal arches
Cardiac muscle origin	Splanchnic mesoderm
Smooth muscle origin	Splanchnic mesoderm (viscera) and local mesenchyme (blood vessels)
HIGH-YIELD EXCEPTION: which muscles are NOT derived from mesoderm?	Smooth muscle of the iris (sphincter and dilator pupillae) and the sweat and mammary glands: these come from ECTODERM
Origin of the iris sphincter and dilator pupillae	Ectoderm, not mesoderm
When do the epimere and hypomere divide?	Week 5
Epimere (dorsal division)	Forms the deep extensor muscles of the back, such as erector spinae; innervated by dorsal primary rami
Hypomere (ventral division)	Forms anterior and lateral body wall muscles (intercostals, obliques, rectus abdominis) and the limb muscles; innervated by ventral primary rami
Which muscles do the dorsal primary rami supply?	Epimere derivatives: the deep extensor muscles of the back
Which muscles do the ventral primary rami supply?	Hypomere derivatives: body wall and limb muscles
Tongue muscles are derived from	Occipital myotomes
Extraocular eye muscles are derived from	Preoptic myotomes
Muscles of mastication, facial expression, pharynx and larynx are derived from	Pharyngeal arch mesenchyme
Teratogen	An environmental agent that causes congenital anomalies
Weeks 1 to 2 (pre-embryonic period)	The all-or-none period: death or total repair
Weeks 3 to 8 (embryonic period)	PEAK SENSITIVITY: organogenesis occurs here, so exposure leads to major structural malformations
Weeks 9 to 38 (fetal period)	Exposure causes functional deficits or minor growth changes
When is the peak teratogen period?	Weeks 3 to 8, the embryonic period
Alcohol	Fetal alcohol syndrome: the number one preventable cause of intellectual disability
Fetal alcohol syndrome features	Microcephaly, flat philtrum, thin upper lip
Number one preventable cause of intellectual disability	Fetal alcohol syndrome
Thalidomide	Severe limb defects: amelia, or phocomelia and meromelia
Amelia	Missing limbs
Phocomelia / meromelia	Seal-like, shortened limbs
Rubella virus	Classic triad of bilateral cataracts, sensorineural deafness and cardiac defects
Nicotine and smoking	Low birth weight and orofacial clefts (cleft lip and palate)
What causes numerical chromosomal anomalies?	Meiotic nondisjunction
Down syndrome	Trisomy 21
Turner syndrome	45,X: the only viable monosomy in humans. Females with short stature, webbed neck and broad chest
Only viable monosomy in humans	Turner syndrome (45,X)
Klinefelter syndrome	47,XXY: male with tall stature, gynecomastia and testicular atrophy
Cri-du-chat syndrome	Terminal deletion of chromosome 5p: cat-like cry and microcephaly
Achondroplasia	Autosomal dominant mutation in FGFR3 causing short-limb dwarfism
Which gene is mutated in achondroplasia?	FGFR3
Structural chromosomal anomaly example	Cri-du-chat syndrome: a terminal deletion of chromosome 5p
