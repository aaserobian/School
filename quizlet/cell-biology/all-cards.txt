The cell	The basic structural and functional unit of life
Cell theory	All organisms consist of one or more cells, and all cells arise from pre-existing cells
Where does function come from?	Molecular and organelle structure
At what level is homeostasis maintained?	The cellular level
Clinical principle of cell theory	Disease is cellular dysfunction made visible
A missing enzyme leads to	A storage disease
A failed junction leads to	A leaky epithelium
The three major cell compartments	Plasma membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus
Plasma membrane	A selectively permeable fluid-mosaic lipid bilayer with embedded proteins
What does the plasma membrane control?	Transport, cell signaling and cell adhesion
Cytoplasm	Cytosol, organelles, cytoskeleton and inclusions
Inclusions	Stored cytoplasmic material such as glycogen or lipid droplets
Nucleus	Enclosed by a nuclear envelope; holds the genome and nucleolus, and is the site of DNA and RNA synthesis
The human genome	22 autosomes plus XX or XY
Cytosol	Site of metabolic pathways and of protein synthesis on free ribosomes
Free ribosomes	Translate mRNA into cytosolic proteins
Rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER)	Studded with ribosomes; translates mRNA into membrane-bound and secreted proteins
Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER)	Synthesizes lipids and steroids, detoxifies substances, and stores calcium
Golgi apparatus	Modifies, sorts and packages proteins and lipids for delivery
Mannose-6-phosphate	The Golgi tag that routes acid hydrolases to the lysosomes
Mitochondria	Generate ATP by oxidative phosphorylation and store calcium
Mitochondrial DNA	Circular, and inherited from the mother only
Lysosomes	Membrane-bound sacs of acid hydrolases for intracellular degradation
Internal pH of a lysosome	About 5
Autophagy	Lysosomal degradation of worn-out organelles
What reaches lysosomes by endocytosis or phagocytosis?	Engulfed extracellular material
Endosomes	Uptake and sorting of material internalized from the cell surface
Peroxisomes	Detoxification and beta-oxidation of very-long-chain fatty acids
Proteasome	Degrades intracellular proteins tagged with ubiquitin, for proteome quality control
Ubiquitin	The tag that marks cytosolic proteins for destruction by the proteasome
Which two organelles store calcium?	The smooth ER and the mitochondria
Microfilaments	About 7 nm across, made of actin
What do microfilaments do?	Cell shape, contraction, motility, and anchoring junctions
Intermediate filaments	About 10 nm across; provide mechanical strength
Intermediate filament tissue markers	Keratin in epithelia, desmin in muscle, GFAP in glia, neurofilaments in neurons
Keratin	The intermediate filament of epithelia
Desmin	The intermediate filament of muscle
GFAP	The intermediate filament of glia
Neurofilaments	The intermediate filaments of neurons
Microtubules	About 25 nm across, made of tubulin
What do microtubules do?	Tracks for organelle transport, the cores of cilia and flagella, and the mitotic spindle
Which drug targets microtubules?	Colchicine
Diameters of the three cytoskeletal elements	Microfilaments about 7 nm, intermediate filaments about 10 nm, microtubules about 25 nm
The secretory pathway, in order	DNA transcribed to mRNA in the nucleus, translated on RER ribosomes, carried by vesicle to the Golgi for modification and packaging, then by vesicle to the plasma membrane or the lysosomes
Step 1 of the secretory pathway	DNA is transcribed to mRNA in the nucleus
Where are secreted proteins translated?	On the ribosomes of the rough ER
What does the Golgi contribute to the secretory pathway?	Modification, sorting and packaging, then dispatch by vesicle
The two destinations of Golgi vesicles	The plasma membrane, for secretion by exocytosis, or the lysosomes
RER-rich cells	Plasma cells and pancreatic acinar cells, built for heavy protein secretion
SER-rich cells	Adrenal cortex, gonads and liver cells, built for lipid and steroid synthesis and detoxification
Which cells are packed with SER, and why?	Adrenal cortex, gonads and liver, because they make steroids and lipids and detoxify
The six functional roles of membrane proteins	Transport, enzymatic activity, signal transduction, cell-cell recognition, intercellular joining, and attachment or anchoring
Mnemonic for membrane protein functions	GET a CHILLED
Channels	Water-filled passive pores, such as aquaporins and ion channels
Carriers (transporters)	Bind solutes and undergo conformational changes; passive or active, such as the GLUT transporters
Pumps (ATPases)	Consume ATP directly to move solutes against a concentration gradient
Channel vs. carrier vs. pump	Channel: a passive pore. Carrier: binds and changes shape. Pump: spends ATP to go against the gradient.
Aquaporins	Channel proteins for water
GLUT transporters	Carrier proteins for glucose
Tight (occluding) junction	The apical seal that prevents paracellular leakage and maintains polarity
Tight junction link proteins	Occludins, claudins and ZO proteins
Tight junction cytoskeletal anchor	Actin
Which pathogen targets tight junctions?	Clostridium perfringens enterotoxin, giving a leaky epithelium and watery diarrhea
Adherens junction	A continuous belt below the tight junctions that stabilizes sheets and initiates cell contact
Adherens junction link proteins	Cadherins, which are calcium-dependent
Adherens junction cytoskeletal anchor	Actin
Desmosome	A spot-weld rivet that resists mechanical shear
Desmosome link proteins	Cadherins
Desmosome cytoskeletal anchor	Keratin intermediate filaments
Which disease targets desmosomes?	Pemphigus vulgaris, giving an intraepidermal blister
Gap junction	Direct cell-to-cell ionic and metabolic communication
Gap junction link proteins	Connexons, built from connexins
Which junction has no cytoskeletal anchor?	The gap junction, because it is pore-forming
Hemidesmosome	Anchors the basal cell surface to the basement membrane
Hemidesmosome link proteins	Integrins
Hemidesmosome cytoskeletal anchor	Keratin intermediate filaments
Which disease targets hemidesmosomes?	Bullous pemphigoid, giving a subepidermal, deeper blister
PemphiGUS vs. Pemphigoid	PemphiGUS hits desmosomes, an intraepidermal blister. PemphigoiD hits hemiDesmosomes, a Deeper subepidermal blister.
Which junctions anchor to actin?	Tight junctions and adherens junctions
Which junctions anchor to keratin?	Desmosomes and hemidesmosomes
Cardiac intercalated discs	Gap junctions for the electrical syncytium, fasciae adherens to transmit contractile force, and desmosomes for mechanical anchoring
Which junction makes the heart an electrical syncytium?	The gap junction
Simple epithelium	One layer, for absorption and secretion
Stratified epithelium	Multiple layers, for protection
Pseudostratified epithelium	A single layer that looks stratified; every cell touches the basement membrane
Transitional epithelium	Stretches to accommodate volume
The three epithelial cell shapes	Squamous (flat), cuboidal (cube), columnar (tall)
Squamous	Flat
Cuboidal	Cube-shaped
Columnar	Tall
Which epithelium lines the urinary bladder?	Transitional
Which epithelium lines the airway?	Pseudostratified ciliated, with goblet cells
Merocrine secretion	Secretion by exocytosis, with the cell left completely intact, as in eccrine sweat glands
Apocrine secretion	The apical portion of cytoplasm is pinched off with the product, as in milk fat secretion
Holocrine secretion	The entire cell ruptures and disintegrates to release its product, as in sebaceous glands
Mnemonic for the secretion modes	MEROcrine is Mere exocytosis. APOcrine is APex pinched off. HOLOcrine is HOLe cell lost.
Which glands secrete by holocrine?	Sebaceous glands
Which glands secrete by merocrine?	Eccrine sweat glands
The four primary tissue types	Epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous
Epithelial tissue	Covers and lines body surfaces and forms glands; minimal extracellular matrix; rests on a basement membrane
Connective tissue	Supports and connects tissues; sparse cells suspended in abundant extracellular matrix
Muscle tissue	Generates force and movement; skeletal, cardiac and smooth
Nervous tissue	Senses stimuli and transmits electrical signals; neurons and glia
Which tissue has the most extracellular matrix?	Connective tissue
Which tissue has minimal extracellular matrix?	Epithelial tissue
Loose (areolar) connective tissue	Few fibers and abundant ground substance; the packing tissue beneath epithelia
Dense irregular connective tissue	Interwoven collagen bundles; the dermis and organ capsules
Dense regular connective tissue	Parallel, densely packed collagen bundles; tendons and ligaments
Reticular connective tissue	A mesh of type III reticular fibers scaffolding lymphoid organs and bone marrow
Which connective tissue is in tendons and ligaments?	Dense regular
Which connective tissue is in the dermis?	Dense irregular
Collagen type I	Bone, skin, tendons and ligaments
Collagen type II	Cartilage
Collagen type III	Reticulin, the reticular fibers of soft organs
Collagen type IV	Basement membrane and basal lamina
Collagen type mnemonic	I is bONE. II is carTWOlage. III is reTHREEculin. IV is under the FLOOR.
A defect in type I collagen causes	Osteogenesis imperfecta
What is vitamin C needed for?	Cross-linking of collagen fibers
Vitamin C deficiency causes	Scurvy
A fibrillin-1 defect causes	Marfan syndrome
Osteogenesis imperfecta	A type I collagen defect: brittle bones and blue sclerae
Marfan syndrome	A fibrillin-1 defect: aortic root dilation and tall stature
Scurvy	Vitamin C deficiency, which blocks collagen cross-linking
How does brown adipose make heat?	UCP-1 dissipates the proton gradient as heat
UCP-1 (thermogenin)	The uncoupling protein of brown adipose tissue that releases the proton gradient as heat
Tay-Sachs disease	A lysosomal storage disorder, GM2 gangliosidosis, from hexosaminidase A deficiency
Which enzyme is deficient in Tay-Sachs?	Hexosaminidase A
Classic eye finding in Tay-Sachs	A cherry-red macular spot
Mitochondrial myopathies	Impaired oxidative phosphorylation causing exercise intolerance, muscle weakness and raised blood lactate
Why do mitochondrial diseases come only from the mother?	Mitochondrial DNA is maternally inherited
