Central nervous system (CNS)	The brain, housed in the cranial cavity, and the spinal cord, housed in the vertebral canal
Peripheral nervous system (PNS)	12 pairs of cranial nerves, 31 pairs of spinal nerves, and ganglia
Ganglion	A cluster of neuron cell bodies in the PNS
The three functional subdivisions of the PNS	Somatic, autonomic and enteric
The three meninges, outermost to innermost	Dura mater, arachnoid mater, pia mater
Mnemonic for the meninges	DAP: Dura, Arachnoid, Pia, from the outside in
What does the subarachnoid space contain?	Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)
Cluster of neuron cell bodies: CNS vs. PNS	Nucleus in the CNS; ganglion in the PNS
Bundle of axons: CNS vs. PNS	Tract in the CNS; nerve in the PNS
Where is the brain housed?	The cranial cavity
Where is the spinal cord housed?	The vertebral canal
Forebrain	The cerebrum (cortex, subcortical basal nuclei, white matter) and the diencephalon
The two parts of the diencephalon	Thalamus, for sensory and motor relay, and hypothalamus, for homeostasis
Thalamus	Sensory and motor relay
Hypothalamus	Homeostasis
Cerebellum	Coordinates posture, balance and motor planning
The three layers of the cerebellar cortex	Granular, Purkinje, molecular
Purkinje cells	The sole output of the cerebellar cortex; GABAergic, therefore inhibitory
The three parts of the brainstem	Midbrain, pons, medulla oblongata
Frontal lobe	Primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus), motor planning, executive function, working memory and Broca's area
Parietal lobe	Primary somatosensory cortex (postcentral gyrus), spatial awareness and integration of sensory inputs
Temporal lobe	Primary auditory cortex, Wernicke's area and memory (hippocampus)
Occipital lobe	Primary visual cortex (V1) and the visual association areas
Precentral gyrus	The primary motor cortex, in the frontal lobe
Postcentral gyrus	The primary somatosensory cortex, in the parietal lobe
Broca's area	Speech production; frontal lobe of the dominant hemisphere
Wernicke's area	Language comprehension; temporal lobe of the dominant hemisphere
Spinal cord gray matter	Medial and butterfly or H-shaped
Dorsal (posterior) horn	Sensory
Ventral (anterior) horn	Motor
Lateral horn (T1 to L2)	Sympathetic preganglionic cell bodies
Spinal cord white matter	Lateral: ascending sensory tracts and descending motor tracts
Gray vs. white matter organization	Brain: gray matter outer (cortex), white matter inner. Spinal cord: gray matter inner and medial, white matter outer and lateral.
Connective tissue layers of a nerve, innermost to outermost	Endoneurium, perineurium, epineurium
Endoneurium	Surrounds an individual axon
Perineurium	Surrounds a fascicle of axons
Epineurium	Surrounds the entire nerve
How many pairs of spinal nerves are there?	31 pairs
Breakdown of the 31 spinal nerve pairs	8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal
Are spinal nerves sensory, motor or mixed?	All spinal nerves are mixed
Which cranial nerves are CNS extensions?	CN I and CN II. CN III to CN XII are PNS nerves.
Sympathetic outflow	Thoracolumbar: T1 to L2
Parasympathetic outflow	Craniosacral: CN III, VII, IX, X and S2 to S4
Sympathetic pathway	Short preganglionic axon to a sympathetic chain ganglion, then a long postganglionic axon releasing norepinephrine
Exception to sympathetic norepinephrine release	Sweat glands, where the postganglionic fiber releases acetylcholine
Parasympathetic pathway	Long preganglionic axon to a terminal ganglion near the target, then a short postganglionic axon releasing acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors
Which nerve carries about 75% of parasympathetic output?	CN X, the vagus nerve
SAME DAVE	Sensory = Afferent, Motor = Efferent; Dorsal = Afferent, Ventral = Efferent
Where do sensory cell bodies sit?	The dorsal root ganglion (DRG)
Multipolar neuron	One axon plus multiple dendrites; the most common type, including motor and cortical neurons
Bipolar neuron	One axon plus one dendrite; retinal, olfactory and vestibulocochlear neurons
Pseudounipolar neuron	A single process that bifurcates into central and peripheral axons; the sensory neurons of the DRG
What morphology are DRG neurons?	Pseudounipolar, both the somatic and the visceral sensory neurons
Glutamatergic neurons	Release glutamate, the primary excitatory neurotransmitter of the CNS
GABAergic neurons	Release GABA; the primary inhibitory interneurons of the brain
Glycinergic neurons	Release glycine; the primary inhibitory interneurons of the spinal cord and brainstem
Chief excitatory neurotransmitter of the CNS	Glutamate
Chief inhibitory neurotransmitters of the CNS	GABA in the brain and glycine in the spinal cord
Soma (perikaryon)	The cell body: nucleus, Nissl bodies and organelles
Nissl bodies	Rough endoplasmic reticulum, where protein synthesis happens
How are neuronal proteins degraded?	By lysosomes and by proteasomes, which are ubiquitin-dependent
What share of the body's oxygen and energy does the brain use?	About 20%
Astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttle	Astrocytes convert glucose into lactate and shuttle it to neurons, which use it to make ATP by oxidative phosphorylation
What energy substrate do astrocytes supply to neurons?	Lactate
Axon initial segment	The trigger zone, where action potentials originate
What do microtubules do in the axon?	Serve as the tracks for fast axonal transport
Anterograde transport	Soma to axon terminal, powered by kinesin
Retrograde transport	Axon terminal to soma, powered by dynein
Kinesin	The motor protein for anterograde transport, soma to terminal
Dynein	The motor protein for retrograde transport, terminal to soma
Electrical synapses	Gap junctions made of connexons: bidirectional, ultrafast, with no synaptic delay
Chemical synapses	Neurotransmitter released into a synaptic cleft: unidirectional, with a delay
Electrical vs. chemical synapses	Electrical: bidirectional, no delay. Chemical: unidirectional, 0.5 to 1 ms delay.
How wide is the synaptic cleft?	20 to 40 nm
How long is the chemical synaptic delay?	0.5 to 1 ms
Ionotropic vs. metabotropic receptors	Ionotropic: fast ligand-gated ion channels. Metabotropic: G-protein-coupled.
Most abundant glial cell of the CNS	Astrocytes
Astrocyte functions	Blood-brain barrier perivascular end-feet, glutamate uptake, potassium regulation, the tripartite synapse, and scar formation by reactive gliosis
How do astrocytes prevent excitotoxicity?	They express EAAT1 and EAAT2 to take up glutamate
Reactive gliosis	Scar formation by astrocytes
Oligodendrocytes	Myelinate CNS axons; one cell myelinates segments of up to about 50 different axons
Schwann cells	Myelinate PNS axons; each cell myelinates a single segment of ONE axon
Myelination: CNS vs. PNS	Oligodendrocyte: one cell to about 50 axons. Schwann cell: one cell to one axon segment.
Microglia	The resident immune and phagocytic cells, about 15 to 20% of brain cells
Where do microglia come from?	Yolk-sac progenitors, so mesodermal, unlike the macroglia, which are ectodermal
Ependymal cells	Ciliated cells lining the ventricles
What produces CSF?	Specialized ependymal cells of the choroid plexus
Satellite cells	Ensheath and support the cell bodies in PNS ganglia
