Pathology | The study of molecular and cellular abnormalities that manifest as disease.
Etiology | The underlying cause of a disease.
Pathogenesis | The step-by-step mechanism by which a disease develops.
Etiology vs. pathogenesis | Etiology is WHY the disease occurs (the cause); pathogenesis is HOW it develops (the mechanism).
Sign | An objective finding observed by a clinician (e.g., fever, a rash, a palpable mass).
Symptom | A subjective experience reported by the patient (e.g., pain, nausea, fatigue).
Diagnosis | Identification of the disease that is currently present.
Prognosis | A forecast of the probable outcome or course of a disease.
Sequelae | Expected later conditions that result from a disease.
Complication | A new, secondary problem that arises from a disease and is not part of its expected course.
Sequelae vs. complications | Sequelae are the expected after-effects of a disease; complications are new secondary problems arising from it.
Acute | Rapid onset and short duration.
Chronic | Gradual onset and prolonged course.
Pathognomonic | A finding so specific to one disease that its presence clinches the diagnosis.
Iatrogenic | A condition caused directly by medical care or treatment.
Biopsy | Removal of a tissue sample for examination by a pathologist.
Cytology | Examination of scattered individual cells rather than intact tissue (e.g., Pap smear, fine-needle aspiration/FNA).
Four diagnostic modalities used in pathology | Biopsy (tissue samples), cytology (scattered cells), laboratory testing (blood and enzyme levels), and imaging.
Suffix -itis | Inflammation.
Suffix -oma | Tumor or neoplasm.
Suffix -osis | A condition, often an abnormal increase.
Suffix -plasia | Growth in the number of cells.
Suffix -opathy | A disease process.
Atrophy | Decrease in cell size and number due to reduced demand or supply (e.g., disuse muscle atrophy, the aging brain).
Hypertrophy | Increase in the size of individual cells (e.g., left ventricular thickening in hypertension).
Why does the hypertensive heart undergo hypertrophy rather than hyperplasia? | Cardiac myocytes are permanent cells that cannot divide, so they can only enlarge.
Hyperplasia | Increase in the total number of cells.
Hypertrophy vs. hyperplasia | Hypertrophy = bigger cells; hyperplasia = more cells.
Metaplasia | Reversible reprogramming of one mature tissue type into another, hardier mature cell type to withstand chronic stress.
Barrett's esophagus | Metaplasia in which the esophageal squamous lining becomes columnar epithelium because of chronic acid reflux.
Is metaplasia reversible? What is the catch? | Yes, it is reversible, but it increases cancer risk (Barrett's esophagus predisposes to esophageal adenocarcinoma).
Cancer that Barrett's esophagus predisposes to | Esophageal adenocarcinoma.
The four reversible cellular adaptations | Atrophy, hypertrophy, hyperplasia, and metaplasia.
Hypoxia/ischemia as a mechanism of cell injury | Oxygen deprivation leads to ATP depletion.
Free radicals as a mechanism of cell injury | Molecules with unpaired electrons damage membrane lipids and nuclear DNA.
First reversible change in cell injury | Cellular swelling, caused by failure of the ATP-dependent Na+/K+ membrane pump.
"Point of no return" in cell injury (irreversible injury) | Severe plasma membrane damage with massive Ca2+ influx into the cell.
Necrosis | Uncontrolled, "messy" death of groups of cells: cells swell, plasma membranes rupture, intracellular contents spill out, and inflammation follows.
Apoptosis | Tidy, energy-requiring programmed "cell suicide" of single scattered cells: cells shrink, bleb, condense their chromatin, and are quietly phagocytosed with no inflammation.
Which type of cell death provokes inflammation? | Necrosis. Apoptosis does not.
Which type of cell death requires energy (ATP)? | Apoptosis.
Cell size in necrosis vs. apoptosis | Necrotic cells SWELL; apoptotic cells SHRINK.
Coagulative necrosis | The classic ischemic infarct pattern (e.g., myocardial infarction); tissue architecture and cell outlines are temporarily preserved.
Why do troponin and CK-MB rise in the blood after a myocardial infarction? | Membrane breakage in necrotic cardiac cells lets intracellular enzymes leak into the bloodstream.
Liquefactive necrosis | Rapid enzymatic digestion of dead tissue; seen in brain infarcts and bacterial abscesses.
Caseous necrosis | Crumbly, "cheese-like" necrosis characteristic of tuberculosis.
Gangrenous necrosis | Ischemic necrosis of a limb or the bowel.
Dry gangrene vs. wet gangrene | Dry = pure coagulative necrosis; wet = coagulative necrosis plus secondary liquefactive necrosis from bacterial infection.
Fat necrosis | Released lipase saponifies fat into chalky deposits; seen in acute pancreatitis and breast trauma.
Fibrinoid necrosis | Smudgy pink vessel walls caused by immune complex deposition or malignant hypertension.
Necrosis pattern of a brain infarct | Liquefactive.
Necrosis pattern of a myocardial infarct | Coagulative.
Necrosis pattern in tuberculosis | Caseous.
Necrosis pattern in acute pancreatitis | Fat necrosis.
Necrosis pattern in a bacterial abscess | Liquefactive.
Necrosis pattern in malignant hypertension or immune complex vasculitis | Fibrinoid.
Semiconservative DNA replication | Each new double helix keeps one original (parental) strand and one newly synthesized strand.
Direction in which DNA polymerase synthesizes new DNA | 5' to 3'.
Phases of the cell cycle in order | G0 (resting) → G1 → S (DNA replication) → G2 → M (mitosis).
G0 phase | Resting phase; the cell is not actively cycling.
S phase | DNA replication (synthesis).
M phase | Mitosis.
Nondisjunction | Failure of chromosomes to separate during cell division, causing aneuploidy (e.g., Trisomy 21).
Aneuploidy | An abnormal number of chromosomes (e.g., Trisomy 21).
Frameshift mutation | An insertion or deletion that shifts the reading frame of a gene.
Change in gene expression vs. mutation | Protein production can be turned down by gene regulation (e.g., reduced lactase in adults) without any change to the underlying DNA sequence.
Neoplasia | "New growth": an abnormal, uncoordinated, autonomous mass of cells that persists even after the original stimulus is removed.
Features of a benign neoplasm | Well-differentiated, slow-growing, encapsulated, non-invasive, and never metastasizes.
Features of a malignant neoplasm | Poorly differentiated or anaplastic, rapid growth with abnormal mitoses, irregular infiltrative borders, locally invasive, and metastasizes.
The single defining feature of malignancy | Metastasis.
Differentiation | The extent to which neoplastic cells resemble their normal tissue of origin.
Dysplasia | Disordered cell growth with variable cell size and shape and abnormal multipolar (tri- or quadripolar) mitotic spindles; reversible and premalignant.
Anaplasia | Complete loss of cell differentiation; a hallmark of high-grade malignancy.
Carcinoma in situ | Severe dysplastic change spanning the full thickness of the epithelium without penetrating the basement membrane.
Benign epithelial tumor nomenclature | Adenoma or papilloma.
Malignant epithelial tumor nomenclature | Carcinoma.
Benign connective tissue tumor nomenclature | Tissue name plus "-oma" (e.g., lipoma, osteoma).
Malignant connective tissue tumor nomenclature | Sarcoma.
Typical route of spread for carcinomas | Lymphatic spread.
Typical route of spread for sarcomas | Hematogenous spread (via blood vessels).
Malignant tumors that break the "-oma means benign" rule | Lymphoma and melanoma are malignant despite the "-oma" ending; leukemia is malignant as well.
Hamartoma | A disorganized mass of tissue native to the site; not a true neoplasm.
Grading | Microscopic assessment of how well the tumor cells are differentiated and how mitotically active they are (low grade vs. high grade/anaplastic).
Staging | Assessment of how far the tumor has spread in the body, using the TNM system.
TNM | T = Tumor size, N = lymph Node involvement, M = distant Metastasis.
Which is the better predictor of clinical prognosis, grading or staging? | Staging.
Grading vs. staging | Grading = what the cells look like under the microscope; staging = how far the tumor has spread in the body.
Four classes of cancer-related genes | Oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes, apoptosis regulators, and DNA repair genes.
Oncogene | A mutated proto-oncogene that drives proliferation; the "accelerator" ("gas pedal stuck").
Number of hits needed to activate an oncogene | One activating mutation.
Tumor suppressor gene | A gene that normally restrains cell division; the "brakes."
Number of hits needed to inactivate a tumor suppressor gene | Two; both copies must be lost ("brakes fail").
p53 | "Guardian of the genome": a tumor suppressor and the most commonly mutated gene in human cancer.
Rb | Retinoblastoma gene; a tumor suppressor ("brake") that restrains cell division.
Apoptosis regulator genes in cancer | Shift the balance between survival and death signals (e.g., overexpressed Bcl-2 prevents cell death).
Bcl-2 | Anti-apoptotic protein; when overexpressed it prevents cell death and lets damaged cells survive.
DNA repair genes | "Spell-checkers" that fix replication errors; their loss produces a mutator phenotype (e.g., HNPCC, xeroderma pigmentosum).
Mutator phenotype | Rapid accumulation of mutations because DNA repair genes have been lost.
Stepwise (multi-hit) model of carcinogenesis | Cancer develops through multi-step clonal evolution; several mutations must accumulate in one cell lineage.
Colon adenoma-to-carcinoma sequence | APC → KRAS → p53.
Why does cancer incidence increase with age? | Multiple mutations ("hits") must accumulate over time before a cell becomes malignant.
The 8 hallmarks of cancer | (1) Sustaining proliferative signaling, (2) evading growth suppressors, (3) resisting apoptosis, (4) enabling replicative immortality, (5) inducing angiogenesis, (6) activating invasion and metastasis, (7) reprogramming energy metabolism, (8) evading immune destruction.
Paraneoplastic syndrome | Clinical effects caused by substances a tumor secretes, rather than by the local mass or its physical spread.
Example of a paraneoplastic syndrome | Small-cell lung carcinoma secreting ectopic ACTH, causing Cushing syndrome.
